What it is, in plain words

Many votes are only valid if enough people took part. A shareholder resolution may need a quorum of the shares to vote. A UK strike ballot is not lawful unless at least 50% of those entitled turn out (Trade Union Act 2016). A works council, an HOA, a pension-trustee board, a standards committee — each has a floor: below it the result does not count, however the votes fell.

That floor is a percentage, and percentages are where hand-counting quietly goes wrong. Someone computes "55 of 95 is… 57.9%, clears 50%" on a calculator, or a spreadsheet does decisive/total*100 in floating-point and a vote that is exactly on the line comes back as 49.99999% and is wrongly thrown out — or 50.00001% and wrongly let through. On a contested resolution, that rounding is what gets challenged.

QuorumFloor removes the arithmetic from the argument. You give it the votes — yes, no, and abstain weights — and the floor as a whole-number percent. It counts yes + no as the decisive votes, counts everyone (abstentions included) as present, and compares them with the single cross-multiplied test decisive × 100 ≥ floor × present — entirely in integers. No division, no float, no drift. The boundary is exact, the tie-break is stated, and the empty ballot is handled on purpose. The code that a mutation gate proves is the code running under your clicks.

How it works — traced from a real run

Take the exact 50/50 boundary preset: yes 25, no 25, abstain 50, floor 50%. Decisive turnout is exactly 50% of those present — right on the line, the hardest case to settle. QuorumFloor cross-multiplies in whole numbers, so it clears by the stated rule (equality passes) with a margin of exactly zero. This particular ratio, 50/100, a float can represent cleanly — but the same whole-number method keeps a boundary like one-third or two-sevenths exact too, and those a float cannot represent, so a floating-point tally can land a hair under the line and fail a vote that actually met its floor. Integers never do. These are the numbers from the live run.

Every edge of that comparison is what the mutation gate attacks: it rewrites >= to > (which would fail the exact boundary), drops the zero-present guard, swaps decisive for present — and each of the 19 rewrites is caught by a test. "19/19 killed" means there is no mutant of this logic the tests fail to notice.

Why integers, not percentages? Because decisive / present × 100 ≥ floor compares two floating-point numbers, and floating-point cannot represent most fractions exactly. The cross-multiplied form decisive × 100 ≥ floor × present uses only whole-number multiplication, which is exact for every input that fits — the boundary cannot wobble.

For the Company Secretary / Governance

Resolution quorums, AGM turnout floors and trustee-board thresholds become exact and reproducible. The margin is explicit, so a contested "did it carry?" is settled by whole-number arithmetic anyone can re-run — not by whose spreadsheet rounded which way.

For the Returning Officer / Ballot runner

Statutory turnout floors (a strike ballot's 50%, a referendum threshold) are judged on the exact line. The kernel never throws, so a malformed entry returns a clean refusal rather than crashing a results run, and the empty and all-abstain cases are handled on purpose.

For the CTO / Eng lead

Drop it into a voting service, a DAO contract's off-chain pre-check, a governance tool or an agent's tool-belt. Zero dependencies, no network, no secrets. Integer-only means bit-identical results across every machine and language you port it to.

For the Risk / Audit lead

The quorum decision is a 60-line pure function with a mutation gate, re-run in public CI — the artefact an auditor can inspect in a sitting. "Passing tests" is cheap; "no mutant survives the tests" is the harder, honest bar you can show a regulator.

White paper — the short version

The problem. A turnout or quorum floor is a percentage threshold, and percentage thresholds are computed with division. Division forces a choice of representation, and the usual choice — floating-point — cannot represent most fractions exactly. On a vote that lands on or near its floor, the rounding direction decides the result, and on a contested vote that rounding is exactly what gets litigated.

The claim. The floor check should be a pure, deterministic, integer-only function with no I/O, so it can run anywhere, be re-run identically by anyone, and be proven. QuorumFloor sums the yes/no/abstain weights, takes yes + no as decisive and the whole as present, and settles the floor with one cross-multiplied comparison, decisive × 100 ≥ floor × present. No division, no float, no drift. Equality clears; a zero ballot never clears. The result is total, honest, and bit-identical on every machine.

Why a mutation gate, not just tests. Passing tests prove the code works on the cases you thought of. A mutation gate proves the tests work: it rewrites the kernel 19 ways — >=→>, the zero-present guard removed, decisive and present swapped — and demands the tests catch every one. 19/19 killed means the suite has no blind spot a bug could hide in. This is the estate's one defensible idea: trust comes from a gate that survives attack, not from a green checkmark.

What it is not. QuorumFloor judges the floor — whether decisive participation reached the required share of those present. It does not decide the outcome: it does not tell you whether yes beat no (that is a separate majority tally, and its sibling QuorumGate weighs a winning choice against a threshold). It does not verify that a voter is who they claim (a signer's job) or that they were eligible to vote (policy). It is designed to compose with those — the referee for the floor, not the whole election.

Risks, answered

"Abstentions shouldn't count toward the floor."They count as present, not decisive — the usual rule; set your weights to your own convention.
"Someone sets the floor too low."Policy, and visible — the floor is in the record and the margin is explicit.
"Weights could be gamed."Weights are your input; gate who can set them upstream, and sign the roster.
"The code could change silently."CI re-runs the gate and the page-parity test; a drifting kernel fails 19/19 or breaks parity.
